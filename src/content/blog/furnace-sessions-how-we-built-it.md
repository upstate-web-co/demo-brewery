The Furnace Sessions started because my buddy Darren had a band and nowhere to play.

That's it. That's the origin story. No grand plan, no market research, no "live music strategy." Darren needed a gig, I had a room with a PA system that the previous tenant left behind, and we figured we'd see what happened.

## The first night

Eight people showed up. Four of them were in the band. One was Darren's mom. The other three were regulars who were already at the bar and didn't realize there would be music.

They played for two hours. The sound was terrible — the brick walls turn everything into echo soup if you don't know what you're doing, and we didn't. But people stayed. A few more walked in off the street when they heard the noise. By the end of the night, we'd sold more beer than any Friday that month.

## What we learned

A few things became obvious fast:

**No cover charge, ever.** The minute you charge at the door, you're a venue. We're not a venue. We're a taproom that happens to have live music. People walk in because the door's open and they hear something — charging them changes the vibe entirely.

**Pay the bands.** We pay every band that plays the Furnace Sessions. Not a lot — we're a taproom, not a concert hall — but something. It's the difference between a favor and a gig, and bands remember which places actually pay.

**Keep it loud enough to feel, quiet enough to talk.** This took months to figure out. The sweet spot is when you can feel the bass in your chest but still order a beer without shouting. We invested in some acoustic panels and a decent sound system. It made all the difference.

**Consistency matters more than talent.** We do this every Friday and Saturday. Every week. Rain, slow nights, holidays. People know that if they show up Friday at 8, there's going to be music. That predictability is worth more than booking the best band once a month.

## How we book

I don't use a booking agent. I go to shows around Greenville, I talk to bands, and I invite the ones I'd want to drink a beer next to. The only requirements:

1. You can actually play your instruments
2. You're fine with people talking over you (it's a bar, not a concert)
3. You'll hang out after and have a beer

That's it. No genre requirements, no demo tapes, no social media follower minimums. Some of our best nights have been bands that nobody had heard of.

## The name

"Furnace Sessions" came from the building. This used to be a railroad warehouse, and there's still an old coal furnace in the back room that we use as decoration. Someone called the Friday night sets "furnace sessions" as a joke, and it stuck.

We leaned into it. The name feels right for what it is — raw, a little rough, heated.

## Why it works for us

The Furnace Sessions are the single best thing we do for business, and we almost never talk about them that way. Here's the math:

Friday and Saturday are already our busiest nights. Adding live music didn't create a new audience — it gave our existing audience a reason to stay longer and bring friends. Average tab goes up. Repeat visits go up. Instagram posts with our name in them go up.

But honestly? I'd do it even if it didn't move the numbers. The best nights at Clinker aren't the ones where we sell the most beer. They're the ones where the band is locked in, the room is full, and nobody's looking at their phone.

That's what a taproom is supposed to feel like.
